## What it covers

| Item | |

| [`RG-020`](../spec/SPEC-SPD-001.en.md#rg-020--journey-totals) | Journey totals, on a sum of one term |

## The data

| Leg | Distance | Speed | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 km | 80.0 km/h | 1.250000 h |

```
total_distance = 100 km
total_duration = 100 ÷ 80 = 1.25 h
average_speed  = 100 ÷ 1.25 = 80.0 km/h
```

**Expected: `average_speed` = 80.0 km/h.**

## Why the case exists

For the **lower bound of the input type**. The specification declares
`legs : Sequence[Leg](1 .. 200)`: one leg is a legal journey, and the boundary of a
declared range is where implementations break.

What it catches is nothing subtle and everything ordinary: an accumulator initialised with
the first element and then looped from index 1, an average divided by `size − 1`, a
`reduce` with no identity value that throws on a single-element stream. None of these
survive a journey of one leg; all of them can survive a journey of two.

It also states an obvious property that is worth stating: **the average speed of a
one-leg journey is the speed of that leg.** If the result is not exactly 80.0, the
arithmetic is wrong somewhere and no other case will tell you where.

## What it does not test

| | |
|---|---|
| Any weighting | One leg cannot be mis-weighted. The naive mean gives 80.0 too |
| The upper bound | `Sequence[Leg](1 .. 200)` also has a top end. No case exercises 200 legs — that is a known hole, and `EX-02` is where it would be tested from |
| The rounding mode | 80.0 is exact |
