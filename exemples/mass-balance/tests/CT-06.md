## What it covers

| Item | |
|---|---|
| [`E-MAS-001`](../spec/SPEC-MAS-001.en.md#e-mas-001) | The target fractions do not add up to exactly 1 → reject |

## The data

`target_batch_mass = 1.000 kg`, `balance_step = 0.001 kg`

| Component | Fraction |
|---|---|
| `CMP-A` | 0.333333 |
| `CMP-B` | 0.333333 |
| `CMP-C` | 0.333333 |

Sum `= 0.999999`, not 1. **Expected: rejected with `E-MAS-001`, before any calculation.
No partial result.**

## Why the case exists

Because the gap is **one millionth**, and everything about the situation invites tolerating
it.

Run the batch anyway and the arithmetic still works: three doses of 0.333 kg, a residual of
0.001 kg, allocated to — whichever component wins a three-way tie — and a final sum of
exactly 1.000 kg. `INV-01` holds. `INV-02` holds. **Every invariant in the specification is
satisfied by a recipe that is not a recipe.** The batch is not what anyone asked for, and
nothing downstream would say so.

That is why the check is a **precondition** and not a rule: it runs before the calculation
and produces no partial result. A recipe whose parts do not make a whole is not a hard
problem to solve, it is a question with no answer.

The comparison is on the exact decimal value, and the implementation earns a comment for
using `compareTo` rather than `equals` — `BigDecimal.equals` tells `1.0` from `1.00`, so a
recipe summing to `1.000000` would be rejected by an implementation comparing with
`equals`. That is a Java trap, not a business rule, and it belongs in the code.

## What it does not test

| | |
|---|---|
| The other direction | Fractions summing to **more** than 1 are equally invalid and no case covers it |
| The tolerance question | Whether a millionth *should* be tolerated is settled by the specification, not asked here |
| Any nominal behaviour | The calculation is never entered |
